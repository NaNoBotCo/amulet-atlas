An erect phallus, modelled in bronze, bone or wood and worn, hung or fixed. Roman versions are frequently winged, and turn up as pendants, finger rings, relief carvings, lamps and wind chimes. Thai palad khik are small carved rods worn at the waist. The sign is not decorative and was not treated as decorative.

The story

In Roman religion the fascinus was the divine phallus. Pliny called it a medicus invidiae — a doctor, or remedy, for envy and for the look that carries it. The Vestal Virgins tended the cult of the fascinus populi Romani, and when a general celebrated a triumph they hung an effigy of it under his chariot to protect him from invidia. Augustine, drawing on Varro, records a phallic image carried in procession at the festival of Father Liber to keep enchantment off the fields.

Who wore one is a narrower question than it looks. There are very few Roman images of people wearing a phallic charm. Varro notes the custom of hanging one at a baby's neck, and phallus-bearing rings have been found too small for anyone but a child. A 2017 experimental archaeology project suggested some types were designed to stay pointing outwards, in the wearer's direction of travel, so as to face whatever was coming.

The explanation Roman writers give is not only fertility. The protective function is usually tied to the regenerative power of an erect phallus, but in most cases what diverts the look is the shame or the laughter that obscenity produces. The victory is sometimes drawn literally: a phallus ejaculating at a disembodied eye, a motif known from reliefs at Leptis Magna in present-day Libya and from several places on Hadrian's Wall, and from a first-century BCE terracotta of two small phallus-men sawing an eyeball in half.

Soldiers carried a compound version, the fist-and-phallus amulet, with a clenched fist at the base of the shaft — often making the fig sign. The largest known collection is from Camulodunum.

The sign is not only Roman. In Thailand the palad khik is carved, blessed and worn at the waist, and Thai practice has its own account of what it is for, which this atlas records in that amulet's own file rather than folding into Rome's.

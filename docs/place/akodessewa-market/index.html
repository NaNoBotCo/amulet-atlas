This one is not fully ours to publish

What is done with the materials sold here belongs to Vodun practitioners, and the sources read for this record do not publish it. This atlas prints the trade and stops. It does not reconstruct a rite from scattered accounts and does not fill the gap with a neighbouring tradition's practice. — this project's own rule, applied because the published sources read for this record describe the market's stock and not its rites

The atlas prints the restriction and stops. Why.

A supply market in the Akodessawa district of Lomé, Togo, for the materials used in Vodun practice. The stock is animal: monkey heads, skulls, dried birds, crocodiles, skins. Sellers work from tables, buyers come with a need, and a practitioner puts the material together for them. The encyclopedia calls it "the world's largest voodoo market", using a spelling this atlas otherwise keeps out of its own sentences.

cited

The story

Vodun is long established in Togo, and the route it took is a loop rather than a line. Enslaved people carried Yoruba gods to the Caribbean and South America; there the gods were joined to Catholic saints and church symbols and changed meaning; when freed people and their families came back to West Africa, they built a Vodun practice in the country their families had left.

The word in the market's French name is the difficult one. A fetish here is comparable to an orisha: it may be a god, or a person, a plant, an animal or a material, depending on the rite and the situation, and in the rite it is activated and strengthened. Some are held to have been particular people.

This atlas does not print rite detail. What a practitioner does with a material bought at these tables is the practitioner's to publish, and the sources read for this record do not publish it. What is on the record is the trade: a district, a named market, sellers, stock, and customers who come for a specific thing.

Inference — the market is photographed more than it is documented. This project found no published price list, no seller interview and no survey it could cite; the record therefore stays with what the encyclopedia states and says plainly that the rest is missing rather than filling it from a travel column.

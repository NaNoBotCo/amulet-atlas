The temple's principal image of Kannon is a hibutsu — a hidden Buddha, never shown to the public. Accounts give it as gold and about 5.5 centimetres high, and say its true details are unknown. The atlas prints the restriction and stops. Why.

Tokyo's oldest-established Buddhist temple, in Asakusa, dedicated to Kannon, the bodhisattva of compassion. You reach the main hall through two gates and a shopping street: the Kaminarimon with its 700-kilogram red lantern, then Nakamise-dōri, 250 metres and about 72 shops, then the Hōzōmon. Omikuji stalls stand inside the precinct and along the approach — shake a numbered stick from a metal canister, read the answer out of one of a hundred drawers.

The story

The temple's origin story is a statue in a net: in 628 two brothers fishing the Sumida pulled up an image of Kannon, and the village headman turned his house into a temple for it. The first documented mention is later, in the Kamakura-period Azuma Kagami, which has Asakusa carpenters called to Kamakura in 1181. Tokugawa Ieyasu made it an official place of prayer for the shogunate in 1590.

The shops came with permission. In 1685 the temple licensed businesses on the main approach in exchange for their cleaning the grounds, and Nakamise has been a selling street ever since — cleared by government order in May 1885, rebuilt in brick that December, flattened by the 1923 earthquake, rebuilt in concrete in 1925, burnt out in the firebombing of 10 March 1945. The main hall went in that raid too and was rebuilt between 1951 and 1958, now under a titanium roof.

The temple is the 13th stop on the Bandō Sanjūsankasho pilgrimage route and the most visited religious site in the world, at over 30 million people a year. For a suggested donation of 100 yen a visitor may consult the omikuji oracle.

Tradition holds — the shops on a temple approach are not a later commercial layer but a continuous trade in selling to pilgrims who walked here, which is the encyclopedia's own framing of Nakamise.

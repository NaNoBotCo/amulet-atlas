Amulet AtlasWords

Amulet, talisman, charm, ta'wiz, omamori, nazar — and what each one actually says.

Arabic (3)

hamsa

A word that is a number. Five — for the fingers — is what the object is called in Arabic, and hamesh, also five, in Hebrew usage.

nazar

A word for a glance that has ended up as the name of a glass disc. Naẓar in Arabic is sight, surveillance, attention.

taweez

The word across the Muslim world for a worn written charm and, by extension, for the object it is folded into: a pendant, letters carved on metal, a framed prayer.

Chinese (1)

fu

960 CE to now

One character, printed on a square of red paper and pasted at the entrance, usually turned upside down.

English (12)

amulet

1450 CE to now

The English cover-word for a protective object, and the word this atlas is written in.

apotropaic

500 BCE to now

The scholar's adjective, and nobody's own. No wearer calls a bead apotropaic.

charm

1300 CE to now

fetish

1610 CE to now

A European word, applied from outside, to objects Europeans met on the West African coast and could not file.

juju

1860 CE to now

luck

1480 CE to now

The word the whole 'good luck charm' category hangs on in English, and a late arrival that came in through a card game.

mascot

1867 CE to now

1920 CE to now

periapt

1500 CE to now

An English word for an amulet worn hung round the neck, defined by the hanging rather than by the shape or the material. It is close to dead.

phylactery

50 CE to now

Three words wearing one spelling, and a reader has to know which is meant.

talisman

1630 CE to now

The word for a made object whose power the maker claims to have put in it.

ward

700 CE to now

The English verb for what the carriers of every object in this atlas say it does. Ward off: fend off, repel, turn aside.

French (1)

gris-gris

A word that names a small cloth sack worn on the person, holding Qur'anic verses written on paper and a set number of small objects.

Japanese (1)

omamori

The Japanese word for a shrine or temple charm, and a rare case of a name that says who is doing the protecting rather than what the thing is made of.

Sanskrit (1)

yantra

1000 BCE to now

A machine. That is the word's plain sense and it has never gone away: yantra is an instrument, a thing built to hold and to do work.

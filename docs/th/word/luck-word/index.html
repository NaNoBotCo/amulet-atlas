ถ้อยคำลัก (luck, โชค)luck

ออกเสียง luck

ชื่ออื่น: luk · gelucke · gelücke · good luck charm

ถ้อยคำบริเตนและไอร์แลนด์ยุโรปตะวันตกอเมริกาเหนือทั่วโลกสหราชอาณาจักรสหรัฐอเมริกาเนเธอร์แลนด์เยอรมนี

คำที่หมวดหมู่ 'เครื่องรางนำโชค' ทั้งหมดในภาษาอังกฤษพึ่งอยู่ และเป็นคำที่มาถึงช้า เข้ามาทางเกมไพ่ บทความในวิกิพีเดียวางเงื่อนไขไว้สามข้อ: โชคมีทั้งดีและร้าย โชคเป็นผลของความบังเอิญ และโชคใช้กับสิ่งมีชีวิตที่มีความรู้สึกนึกคิด ไม่มีอะไรในนิยามนี้เกี่ยวข้องกับพระเจ้า และนั่นคือสิ่งที่เปลี่ยนไปเมื่อคำนี้มาถึง

อ้างอิง

Middle Low German, Dutch or Frisian luk, a short form of gelucke (Middle High German gelücke) > English luck
บันทึกครั้งแรก: the 1480s in English (Wikipedia's Luck article)
It likely entered English as a gambling term, and the context of gambling stays detectable in the word's connotations. Before it, Old and Middle English said speed (Old English spēd, Middle English spede) for good fortune — a word of fate or divine help, not of chance: 'Christ be our speed', in William Robertson's Phraseologia generalis of 1693. Chance itself is a Latin loanword through Old French cheance, from Late Latin cadentia, 'falling', taken into Middle English from the late thirteenth century. Wikipedia's article also compares old Slavic lukyj and old Russian luchaj. อ้างอิง

เรื่องราว

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

English managed without it until the 1480s. What it had instead was speed — Old English spēd — which meant good fortune and also prosperity, profit and abundance, and which named help rather than chance. A bestower of success could be called a speed. Then luck came in from Low German, Dutch or Frisian, probably at a gaming table, and it named something else: a personal chance event, with nobody behind it.

The idea it needed had arrived a couple of centuries earlier. Chance, from Old French cheance and Late Latin cadentia, literally describes an outcome as a falling — as of dice — and English took it from the late thirteenth century. Fortuna, the Roman goddess, was a popular medieval allegory, and learned writers of the High Middle Ages made her a servant of God distributing success and failure in a fickle way, which is how chance was fitted into a Christian frame without being given a will of its own.

The symbols the word gathers are local and contradictory. Seven is a lucky number in Christian-influenced cultures, eight in Chinese-influenced ones. In Chinese and Japanese, four is unlucky because it sounds like the word for death. Greek practice counts entering and leaving a house by different doors, and breaking a mirror, as unlucky; Navajo practice counts throwing rocks into a whirlwind. In Western practice, opening an umbrella indoors may be counted unlucky partly because it can poke somebody in the eye, and shaking hands with a chimney sweep counted lucky partly because it is a kind but unpleasant thing to do.

The gods stayed on for a while beside it: Fortuna and Felicitas in Roman religion, Dedun in Nubian religion, the Seven Lucky Gods in Japan, Lakshmi and her inauspicious counterpart Alakshmi in Hinduism.

Inference — the arrival of luck is the arrival of the idea that the outcome is nobody's decision. An object bought against that is a different object from one bought to petition somebody, even when it is the same object on the same string. Half the arguments in this atlas about whether a charm is devotional or commercial are really arguments about which of those two words the buyer is using.

เครื่องรางเสาเจดDjed pillar𓊽

ออกเสียง JED, anglicised; Ancient Egyptian ḏd, Coptic ϫⲱⲧ jōt, 'pillar'

ชื่ออื่น: djed · djt · backbone of Osiris · pillar of stability

เครื่องรางอียิปต์โบราณอียิปต์ความมั่นใจ กลางรอตรวจสอบ

เสาต้นหนึ่งที่มีแถบแบนสี่แถบซ้อนขวางอยู่ที่ยอด เหมือนหัวเสาที่มีปลอกคอมากเกินไป เมื่อเป็นเครื่องราง จะมีขนาดเล็กและห้อยคอ คัมภีร์แห่งความตาย (Book of the Dead) กำหนดให้ใช้เสาเจดทองคำสำหรับมัมมี่ นอกจากนี้ยังวาดไว้บนโลงศพ และแกะสลักด้วยไม้ขนาดเท่าจริงสำหรับพิธีที่ยกเสาขึ้นตั้งตรง

อ้างอิง

Egyptian ḏd 'pillar', surviving into Coptic as ϫⲱⲧ jōt. The sign writes the word for stability.
Egyptologists read the object it draws as Osiris's spine. Erich Neumann read it instead as a tree, and pointed out that Egypt was largely treeless, so the myth may be carrying the memory of timber imported from Syria. Gordon and Schwabe, in 2004, argued it is a bull's sacrum. อ้างอิง

หมายเหตุThe source dates the djed's firm attachment to Osiris to the New Kingdom and does not date the amulet itself. The New Kingdom's span, c. 1550–1069 BCE, is taken from s:wp-taweret. *Inference* — the amulet is certainly older than this window, because the sign is called one of the more ancient in Egyptian religion and the Osiris raising is described from Dynasty XII onward; a window that can be sourced is entered here rather than a guess that cannot.

เรื่องราว

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

The djed is a spine that had to be set up again. In the Osiris myth, Set tricks Osiris into a coffin cut to fit him exactly and throws it in the Nile; it drifts to Byblos, a tree grows around it, the king of the land has the tree cut and stood in his palace as a pillar. Isis, searching, gets herself into the royal household, asks for that pillar, takes the coffin out of it, and then anoints the wood with myrrh and wraps it in linen. That is the pillar of djed.

So the ceremony is a resurrection staged as carpentry. At the Sed festival, the pharaoh with priests pulling on ropes hauls a wooden djed column upright at Memphis. It was read as Osiris's win over Set. The timing is agricultural: the month of Koiak, the fourth month of the Season of the Inundation, when the fields are being sown, inside the Osiris festival that ran from the thirteenth to the thirtieth of that month. A relief in Amenhotep III's Luxor temple shows the raising at his third Sed festival, in his thirty-seventh regnal year: the pillar stands in a shrine with a human body under a djed head, wedjat eyes, crook and flail, the feather crown and the sun disc; the king presents a loaded offering table; below, food-bearers weave between heavy-stepping dancers, singers give a short hymn to Ptah, and men box and do a stick dance. Sigrid Hodel-Hoenes, who describes the scene, reads the young women rattling Hathor sistra as the king's daughters, from the line of hieroglyphs above their heads: 'Children of the king praising (or charming) the noble djed pillar.'

The amulet is the private end of the same idea. It goes near the spine of a mummified body to make the resurrection happen, and the Book of the Dead gives a spell to be spoken over a gold djed hung at the mummy's neck: it promises the mummy will get the use of its spine back and be able to sit up.

Tradition holds — the sign starts somewhere agricultural, in a pillar of reeds or sheaves with grain piled round it, and only later attaches to Ptah and then to Osiris. Ptah was called 'the noble djed' and carried a sceptre that fused the djed with the ankh. By the New Kingdom the sign belongs to Osiris.

อ้างอิง

ทำและพกอย่างไร

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

Cut or moulded small and hung at the neck, laid near the spine inside the wrappings, or painted on the coffin. The Book of the Dead's spell is spoken over a gold one. At festival scale it is a wooden column raised by ropes, with the king pulling and priests supporting it.

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

Not made and not raised; the ceremony ended with the religion. The sign survives as a museum label and as a design in jewellery sold to Egypt's visitors. As of 2026 this project has read no source describing anybody carrying a djed for what the Book of the Dead promised it would do.

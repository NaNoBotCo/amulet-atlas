Japanese 合格祈願 gōkaku kigan, from 合格 gōkaku 'passing an examination' and 祈願 kigan 'prayer, petition'
The commercial category is gōkaku kigan shōhin, goods for the prayer to pass. The Kit Kat leg of it runs on a different mechanism: きっと勝つとぉ kitto katsutoo, a Kyushu phrase meaning 'you shall surely win', which is what キットカット Kitto Katto sounds like. อ้างอิง

เรื่องราว

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

Japan's examination charm has a god, and he was a real man. Sugawara no Michizane was a Heian-period scholar and poet who was exiled to Kyushu and died there; he is enshrined as Tenjin, god of scholarship and learning — and of natural disasters, which is a detail worth keeping. Dazaifu Tenmangū, the shrine built where he was exiled, states it plainly on its own English page: "As Tenjin is the deity of learning, amulets for academic success are one of the most popular items to take home from Dazaifu Tenmangu." Many of the amulets carry the plum flower, which the shrine says Michizane loved and which is its symbol. The shrine's own framing of the exchange is careful and worth quoting as printed: "At Shinto shrines you can receive an omamori, or amulet, to bring good luck, for a small donation." Received, for a donation. Not bought.

The omamori for this is gakugyō-jōju, education and passing examinations, one of a standard list that also runs to traffic safety, safe childbirth, a good marriage, business prosperity and family peace. The rule is the same as for every omamori: do not open it, and return the old one to the shrine it came from — traditionally to be burnt rather than binned, out of respect to the deity that carried you through the year.

The second Japanese object is the Kit Kat, and the wordplay has to be got right. キットカット, Kitto Katto, is what the brand is called in Japanese. Kitto means surely. Katsu means to win. The Kyushu phrase is きっと勝つとぉ, kitto katsutoo, 'you shall surely win', and that is the thing the brand name sounds like. Peter Davis, writing for Japan House at the University of Illinois, gives the figure: "In fact, one in three students in Japan will buy a Kit-Kat in anticipation of entrance examinations." The English Wikipedia article is more guarded and says the marketing "is believed to have benefited from the coincidental false cognate", and that market research shows the brand strongly correlated with good-luck charms among students ahead of exams. Inference — the two accounts differ on how much of this Nestlé made and how much of it Nestlé found, and this project does not have the internal documents to settle that. What is dated is the marketing: the 'Lucky Charm' campaign won the Asian Brand Marketing Effectiveness Award in 2005, and in 2009 Nestlé and Japan Post ran a campaign letting people write on the bars and post them from 20,000 post offices — which won the Media Grand Prix at Cannes Lions in 2010.

Korea works the same joke on a different verb. Families and friends give a student 엿, yeot — a taffy boiled down from steamed rice, glutinous rice, sorghum, corn or sweet potato — and 찹쌀떡, chapssaltteok, glutinous rice cake, before the Suneung university entrance examination. Both are sticky. The Korean verb for sticking to something is also the verb for passing an exam, so the gift says what it means without saying it. The English Wikipedia entry on yeot confirms students receive it before examinations and otherwise concentrates on the same word's career as an obscenity, which is the other thing a Korean reader knows about it.

The shrine end of the trade has been argued about in public, with a date attached. In 1979 two shrines dedicated to education sold ema — the wooden prayer plaques — for examination success; after public outcry, the funds were turned into a scholarship in 1980. Inference — that is the cleanest instance this atlas has of a charm trade answering a complaint by converting the takings into the thing the charm was asking for.

อ้างอิง

ทำและพกอย่างไร

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

An omamori is received at the shrine office for a donation, carried on the person or tied to a bag, not opened, and returned to the same shrine at the New Year to be burnt. An ema is bought at the shrine, written on, and hung on the rack until it is ritually burnt at a ceremony — the burning treated as freeing the wish. A Kit Kat is bought at a convenience store, sometimes posted, and eaten. Yeot and chapssaltteok are given by family and friends and eaten. Nothing consecrates the last two, and nobody claims anything does.

ปัจจุบัน

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

Running every winter. Japanese entrance-examination season and the Korean Suneung both fall in the cold months, and the shelves change accordingly. As of 2026 the shrine goods, the exam-season chocolate packaging and the taffy all coexist, with the shrine goods carrying a deity's name and the other two carrying a pun. No dated price was read for any of them, and neither Dazaifu Tenmangū's page nor the sources on the Korean gifts print one.

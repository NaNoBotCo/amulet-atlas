เรื่องราว

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

The woman on the pendant is Abyzou, the demon blamed for miscarriage and infant mortality, and said to be driven by envy because she is infertile herself. She is Alabasandria in Coptic Egypt and Gylou in Byzantine Greek, and in the texts she has more names than anyone can count. In the Testament of Solomon she tells the king she never sleeps, that she walks the world looking for women about to give birth, and that she will strangle a newborn if she gets the chance.

The amulets take the healing-charm grammar of the late antique Mediterranean — address the affliction by name and order it out — and apply it to her. One bronze amulet labels the standing figure Arlaph and reads "The Seal of Solomon [is] with the bearer; I am Noskam." The reverse, written inside the ouroboros, reads: "Flee, flee, Abyzou, [from] Sisinios and Sisinnia; the voracious dog dwells here."

Who does the driving off varies: Solomon, or Arlaph — called Afarof in the Testament of Solomon and identified with the archangel Raphael — or, on Christian examples, Saint Sisinnios. On one medieval piece the rider is Sisinnios, the demon is named twice over as Abizou and Anabardalea, and an angel called Araph stands by with one wing raised.

The same scene exists in paint. At the monastery of St Apollo in Bawit, in Egypt, a wall painting shows the childbirth demon named Alabasandria trampled under a horse; the rider wears a belted tunic and trousers in the Parthian style, and the inscription, faded by the time it was read, was read as Sisinnios. Round the central image are a centaur, the piercing of the evil eye, and the demon's winged, reptile-tailed daughter, named in her own inscription.

Inference — the amulets are usually described as protecting women in childbirth and their infants, and Jeffrey Spier's survey notes that some of the names of the people seeking protection on surviving examples are men's. Whatever these pendants were understood to be doing, they were not only for mothers.

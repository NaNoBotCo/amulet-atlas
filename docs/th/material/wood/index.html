วัสดุไม้Wood絵馬

ออกเสียง ema, 'picture-horse', the wooden plaque hung at a shrine

ชื่ออื่น: timber · board · slate · kifuda · carved figure

วัสดุญี่ปุ่นบริเตนและไอร์แลนด์แอฟริกาตะวันตกแอฟริกากลางเมโสอเมริกาอเมริกาเหนือสแกนดิเนเวียและยุโรปเหนือญี่ปุ่นสหราชอาณาจักรไนจีเรียแคเมอรูนกานาคองโก (กินชาซา)เม็กซิโกสหรัฐอเมริกานอร์เวย์สวีเดนฟินแลนด์

ตัด แกะ ผ่า หรือไส ไม้น่าจะเป็นวัสดุเครื่องรางที่พบมากที่สุดในโลก และเป็นหนึ่งในวัสดุที่หายากที่สุดในถาดพิพิธภัณฑ์ที่เก็บของอายุเกินสองสามร้อยปี เพราะไม้ไหม้ไฟ ผุพัง และในธรรมเนียมจำนวนมาก ก็ตั้งใจให้เป็นเช่นนั้น

เรื่องราว

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

Japan writes on it and then burns it. Ema are small wooden plaques, typically 15 cm wide and 9 cm tall, on which Shinto and Buddhist worshippers write prayers or wishes and hang them at the shrine, where they stay until they are ritually burned at special events — the burning understood as releasing the wish. The substitution behind them is documented: horses were held to carry messages from the kami, live horses were expensive, and a wooden statue or painted plaque could stand in as a katashiro, a substitute effigy. The earliest text record of such a substitution is the Honcho Bunsui of 1013, recording an offering of three paper horses at the Kitano Tenjin shrine, and clay or wood horse figures survive from the Nara period.

Japan also keeps a sliver of it. Ofuda are made of paper, wood, cloth or metal, and the wooden ones are called kifuda; the best-known form of the Jingū Taima is a wooden tablet containing a sliver of cedar known as the gyoshin, the sacred core, wrapped in paper printed with the shrine's name and stamped with its seals. A document of 1777 indicates that between eighty-nine and ninety per cent of households in the country owned an Ise talisman at the time.

West Africa writes on it and then washes it. The Qur'an board is a wooden tablet of locally available woods such as mahogany or baobab, on which a student copies verses; when the passage is mastered the writing is washed off and the water is collected and drunk or used in healing.

Central and West Africa carve it. An nkondi is most often a carved human figure with open cavities in its body — most commonly in the belly, but also on the head or in pouches at the neck — for the bilongo, the medicines that make it the home of a spirit, usually hidden behind resin-fixed mirrors; people drive nails into the figure as a petition for help, healing or witness, and the nailing is described as waking and sometimes enraging it. Akua'ba are wooden fertility dolls with large disc-like heads, from western Ghana and particularly associated with the Fanti, used by young women seeking to conceive; when not in use they are ritually washed and cared for in the homestead.

Europe's protective wood has a species. The rowan, Sorbus aucuparia, is a small deciduous tree 4 to 12 metres tall; its wood is dense and used for carving, turning, tool handles and walking sticks. James Frazer reported in 1890 a Scottish tradition of planting it near a gate or front door, and Thomas Keightley recorded that mortals could safely watch the fairy rades by placing a rowan branch over their doors. In the Highlands there were strong taboos against using any part of the tree save the berries, except for ritual: a threshing tool of rowan called a buaitean was used on grain meant for rituals and celebrations. The Old English name cwic-bēam survives as quickbeam, and by the nineteenth century had been reinterpreted as connected to the word witch, producing wicken-tree, wich-tree, wicky and wiggan-tree.

North America bends it. A dreamcatcher is a handmade willow hoop with a net woven across it; Frances Densmore recorded in 1929 that mothers and grandmothers wove webs for children using willow hoops and sinew or plant cordage, and that in old times the netting was made of nettle fibre. Mexico sells crosses of ocote wood for good luck at the Mercado de Sonora.

Inference — look at what those examples have in common. The ema is burned, the ofuda is replaced yearly, the slate is washed, the dreamcatcher's hoop is green wood. This material is chosen for traditions with an expiry date built into them, and a museum collects the ones that failed to expire.

Carved, split, planed into a board, or bent green into a hoop. The Kongo case is the most explicit about who: an nkondi is built by an nganga, often isolated in a hidden camp away from the village, and the figure is composed at the edge of the village because the edge and the entrances are what need guarding.

ปัจจุบัน

ส่วนนี้ยังไม่ได้เขียนเป็นภาษาไทย ด้านล่างเป็นภาษาอังกฤษ

Akua'ba are now more commonly seen as mass-produced works of art or souvenirs than as heirlooms in ritual use, though traditional use continues in some areas among the Fante and other Akan peoples, and the form has become a symbol of good luck more broadly. Europeans visiting Africa in the 1800s were keenly interested in Fante dolls, which is why so many are in European museums. Japan's ema have their own commercial argument: two shrines dedicated to education sold ema for examination success in 1979 and, after public outcry, turned the funds into a scholarship in 1980.

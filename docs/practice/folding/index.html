This one is not fully ours to publish

What is inside a sealed packet is, in several of these traditions, not the carrier's information and not this project's. Omamori are customarily not opened; Taoist talismanic craft is transmitted in secret. This record describes the fold and what it is for, and does not describe the contents of packets a tradition keeps shut. — the omamori custom as reported in the cited account; Taoist transmission as described by Ge Zhaoguang of Fudan University

The atlas prints the restriction and stops. Why.

The step after the writing: the sheet is rolled into a tube, folded into a packet, or creased into a shape, and then cased, sewn or sealed. From that point the text is inside and nobody reads it. A takrut is a rolled scroll in a tube; a mezuzah's parchment is rolled and dropped into its case; a Japanese kenharai is a sliver of wood inside a fold of paper cut so the packet resembles a sword blade.

The story

Two silver scrolls from a burial chamber at Ketef Hinnom, near Jerusalem, were found rolled in 1979 and took three years to unroll without disintegrating. They carry the Priestly Blessing and date to around 600 BCE. Whoever wore them never read them either: the text went in, the metal closed, and it stayed closed for twenty-six centuries.

Japan folded the same logic into a distribution system. Wandering preachers attached to the shrines of Ise, the oshi, handed out purification wands called Oharai Taima to households across the country as proof that prayers had been said on their behalf. The wands travelled either in folded paper packets — kenharai, named because the packet's shape resembles a sword blade, ken — or in boxes, oharaibako. Distribution began in the Muromachi period and peaked in the Edo: a document of 1777 records that between eighty-nine and ninety per cent of households in the country owned an Ise talisman. Kenharai are still given out at Ise.

The Greco-Roman world folded to bind. Lead curse tablets were rolled, folded or pierced with nails before they went into the ground, and some love spells have been found folded around a piece of the target's hair, which the tablets' own texts treat as carrying that person's ousia, their central essence.

The seal creates a rule of its own. Omamori are customarily not opened, so that the protection is not lost — the carrier holds a brocade bag whose contents are the shrine's business, not theirs. Inference — that single custom is the clearest statement in this atlas of what a fold is for. Once the packet is closed, the object stops being a text a person can check and becomes a thing a person has to trust. Scribal traditions and sealed-packet traditions split exactly here: a mezuzah is opened and inspected by a scribe twice in seven years, and an omamori is never opened at all.

How it is made and carried

Roll, or fold, then contain. Metal foil rolls around a former and slides into a tube or a case. Parchment rolls and goes into a mezuzah case or a tefillin box. Paper folds into a packet that is itself the shape of something — a blade, in the Ise case. Lead folds over on itself, sometimes around hair or a scrap of clothing, and can then be nailed through. Cloth versions are folded and sewn shut rather than cased, which crosses into sewing-in.

cited

Now

Live and industrial. Shrines and temples across Japan issue folded and cased talismans year-round, with the Association of Shinto Shrines distributing Jingū Taima to affiliated shrines nationally. Thai takrut are still rolled and cased and worn at the neck or the waist. Where a sealed charm is sold to somebody outside the tradition, the seal is also what makes it unverifiable, which is the trade's oldest complaint about itself.

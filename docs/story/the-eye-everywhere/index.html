storiesThe eye everywhere

also: is the eye universal · one eye or many

storieEverywhereMesopotamiaGreece and the AegeanAnatoliaSouth AsiaMesoamericaBrazilAncient EgyptIraqGreeceTurkeyItalyIndiaMexicoPuerto RicoBrazilEgyptSpainPortugal

An eye is drawn, moulded, beaded or painted on protective objects from the Tigris to Tijuana, and the temptation is to call that a single human idea. It is not one thing. Some of it is documented transmission with a route and a direction. Some of it is one tradition swapping one startling shape for another inside its own borders. Some of it is two things that look alike and are not related. And in at least one important case the best scholarship says the question cannot be settled. This record goes case by case and says which is which.

The claim, stated exactly

Amulets against a harmful look have been found dating to around five thousand years ago. The belief is documented across the Mediterranean, the Balkans, Eastern Europe, West Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, Africa, the Caribbean and Latin America, and it recurs throughout Jewish rabbinic literature. That is a very large range, and it is the fact that makes the eye the most widespread sign in this atlas.

It is not, however, a claim that every culture arrived at it. The published summary this project read is specific about the limit: belief in the evil eye likely originated within Indo-European, Mediterranean and West Asian cultures, probably starting in Sumeria, and spread through the Greco-Roman world, India, Portugal, Spain, northern Europe and North Africa. And then the sentence that does the real work: it has not been found in the original folklore traditions of Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa and Native America.

So the headline is not "everywhere". It is "one very old idea with a traceable spread, plus a lot of things that resemble it".

Transmission you can follow

The clearest line runs through glass. After glass-bead production got going in the Mediterranean around 1500 BCE, blue-and-white concentric eye beads spread among Phoenicians, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Arabs and Ottomans — one technology, one shape, carried by traders along routes that are themselves documented. The bead a shop in Istanbul or Athens sells in 2026 is the descendant of that run, and the countries where the nazar is still hung in homes, offices, cars and children's clothing make a continuous block: Turkey, Romania, Albania, North Macedonia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Greece, Cyprus, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel, Palestine, Egypt, Armenia, Iran, India, Pakistan, Uzbekistan, Afghanistan, Turkmenistan, Iraq and Azerbaijan.

The classical world argued about the mechanism rather than the existence. Plutarch gives the subject a chapter of his Symposium, and explains it physically: the eyes were the chief, if not the sole, source of deadly rays that sprang up like poisoned darts from the inner recesses of a person possessing the evil eye. He also treats the whole phenomenon as a source of wonder and a cause of incredulity, which is to say classical authors were not unanimous either. Pliny the Elder writes of African enchanters having the "power of fascination with the eyes" and being able to kill those on whom they fix their gaze.

The Iberian route to the Americas is the best-attested single transmission in the set. The evil eye — mal de ojo — is deeply embedded in Spanish popular culture, and Spain is named as the origin of the belief in Latin America. Only with the arrival of the Spanish colonies did it spread there.

And the drawn Egyptian eye has its own line: the wedjat runs from the Old Kingdom to Roman Egypt, and Syria, Canaan and especially Nubia took the sign up from Egypt. That is transmission too — but of a sign for soundness and healing, in a religion with its own account of it, and reading the Mediterranean eye-against-the-eye logic back into pharaonic Egypt is a later gloss. This atlas keeps the two apart and says so on the wedjat record.

Substitution inside one tradition, which is not the same thing

Where the eye is not drawn, something else stands in, and the substitutes are the most interesting evidence in the file.

Illyrians used shapes of the phallus, the hand, the leg and animal teeth against the same harm. Romans used the fascinum, a winged phallus. Southern Italy still uses the cornicello, the cimaruta and the sign of the horns. In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana the harm is called disti or drusti; in Tamil Nadu and Kerala drishti or kannu; in Karnataka drushti — and it is removed by rotating rock salt, red chillies, a white pumpkin, an oiled cloth or a lemon coated with kumkuma around the affected person and then burning or discarding the item. Pictures of fierce ogres, drishti bommai, hang in houses and vehicles for the same purpose. Indians leave patches of rock salt outside the door and hang green chillies, neem leaves and lemons on the stoop.

Inference — none of these is an eye, and all of them are doing the eye's job. What they share is that each is a thing that catches a glance. If the mechanism your tradition describes is interception, then anything startling enough to take a look will serve, and the shape is free to vary. That is why this atlas files the horn, the hand, the fist and the phallic sign as kin of the eye rather than as unrelated signs — not because they look alike, but because the people using them give the same reason.

Resemblance that is not relation

Some eyes are not in the family at all, and saying so is the point of the exercise.

The Greek eye-cup is a case of the sign doing local work: exaggerated eyes were painted on drinking vessels, the kylikes, from the sixth century BCE to the end of the classical period, possibly to stop evil spirits entering the mouth while drinking. That is inside the Mediterranean tradition. The Chinese bagua mirror, installed to turn away negative energy at the entrance of a residence, is not — it is a reflective object in a different cosmology, doing a job that reads as parallel and has no documented line back to Sumeria. Eyes painted on the bows of Mediterranean fishing boats are family. Eyes painted on boats elsewhere may not be, and this atlas does not assume.

Brazil is the sharpest test, because the belief arrived there and then changed shape. Brazilians associate mau-olhado, or olho gordo, with envy directed at garden plants that weaken and die after a certain visitor, and at attractive hair — and, unlike most cultures in the range, do not treat it as a risk to young babies. Unbaptised children are instead held to be at risk from bruxas, witches with intention of their own, probably reflecting Galician folktales about meigas carried over by Portuguese settlers. The protections are mirrors on the front door, an elephant figurine with its back to it, and coarse salt in particular places. Same ancestor, different creature.

The case nobody can settle

Mexico is where the answer is "unknown", and it is worth giving the reason rather than the verdict.

The historian Alfredo López Austin notes that it appears unviable to determine whether there are Indigenous or Spanish influences in Mexican mal de ojo. Evidence from the colonial period is thin and contradictory. The Spanish, whether out of unfamiliarity with Nahua belief or out of a dismissive attitude to it, misunderstood and misrepresented Indigenous rituals around witchcraft, and of all those practices the only one bearing a faint resemblance to the European idea is the notion of envy and the damage it can do.

What Mexico does have is a distinct local shape. There, unlike elsewhere, mal de ojo is held to be caused by a glance even without jealousy, envy or ill intention on the looker's part. The ethnologist and historian Noemí Quezada, reading records of the Mexican and Spanish Inquisitions, finds that healers concentrated their treatments on children, whose bodies were seen as particularly vulnerable.

In Puerto Rico the protective object is the azabache bracelet, traditionally carrying a black or red coral amulet in the shape of a fist with the index finger knuckle protruding — a gesture frozen in stone. Admirers touching an infant is also held to dispel it, which is the same remedy recorded in Mexico and Central America, where infants are considered at special risk and are given an amulet bracelet, typically with an eye-like spot painted on it.

The tally

Inference — set out as four verdicts, with the confidence this project can actually carry:

Documented transmission: Sumeria to the Greco-Roman world to Iberia to Latin America; the glass eye bead from the Mediterranean outward after c. 1500 BCE; the Egyptian wedjat into Nubia and the Levant. Strong.

Substitution inside a tradition: the Roman fascinum, the Illyrian hand and tooth, the southern Italian horn, the South Asian lemon-and-chilli. Strong, and the reason given by carriers is consistent across all of them.

Parallel but unrelated: the bagua mirror and comparable reflective wards outside the documented range. This project treats them as parallels, files them as such, and does not claim a link.

Unknown: Mexico, on López Austin's own statement. Left unknown, on the page, in those words.

The last one is the most useful entry in the record. An atlas that can say "undetermined" about its most famous sign is an atlas a reader can trust about the rest.

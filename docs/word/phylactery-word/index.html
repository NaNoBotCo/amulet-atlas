also: phylacteries · phylaktērion · phylaktēria

Three words wearing one spelling, and a reader has to know which is meant. In Bible translation and in older Jewish scholarship, phylactery is the English word for tefillin — the leather boxes of Torah text bound to arm and head. In general English it is a loose synonym for any amulet. In tabletop gaming it names the object a lich hides its life force in, which is where most English speakers under forty meet the word first.

The story

The Greek starts as architecture and police work. Phylaktērion is a guarded post, a safeguard, a security; only in later Greek does it mean an amulet. The New Testament uses it once, at Matthew 23, for the boxes worn by Jews — a Greek word for a Jewish object, chosen by a Greek-writing author — and from that single occurrence it entered the European languages. Tefillin itself is a different word with a different history: the Targumim and the Peshitta are the first texts to use it, and Jastrow connects it to a Syriac word for attachments or hangings.

The archaeology and the word meet: finds at Qumran and the reference in Matthew 23 together show that by the first century CE many Jews were wearing some physical form of the object.

The third stage is datable and recent. The term appears in the first-edition Advanced Dungeons & Dragons Monster Manual in 1977, where a lich holds its status by conjurations, enchantments and a phylactery, and the nature and function of that phylactery is not explained. By the second edition in 1989 the object explicitly stores the lich's life force. The third-edition Monster Manual, in 2000, describes it as resembling a Jewish tefillin, and notes it can also be a ring or an amulet.

That borrowing has been argued with. Eric Silver has criticised the gaming term for its Jewish religious origin and proposed talisman as the neutral word instead. Wizards of the Coast has not retired the term; Paizo Publishing stopped using it in 2021, and its liches now keep their souls in a soul cage.

Inference — the arc is one word carried three times by people who were not its owners: a Greek writer naming a Jewish practice, European translators keeping his choice, and a game designer reaching past both for a container that sounded old.

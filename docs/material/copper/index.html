Copper and its alloys: bronze with tin, brass with zinc. It casts, it takes an inscription, it corrodes to a green skin and then stops, and it was cheap enough to make a charm by the thousand in places where silver was not. Where iron rusts away and lead poisons, copper mostly just sits in the ground and waits.

The story

China cast the most of it. Yansheng coins — Chinese numismatic charms — originated in the Western Han as variants of the Ban Liang and Wu Zhu cash coins, and were valuable in themselves because they were often made of copper alloys and Chinese coins were valued by their weight in bronze or brass. Supply drove the practice: temple coins were issued by Buddhist temples during the Yuan dynasty when the copper currency was scarce, or when copper production was intentionally limited by the Mongol government.

The measurements exist because these were struck to a standard. "Laid to rest" burial charms are bronze, 2.4 to 2.45 centimetres across and 1.3 to 1.4 millimetres thick, carrying the inscription rù tǔ wéi ān, "to be laid to rest", with a blank reverse. Most come from late Qing graves, and one turned up in a hoard of Northern Song coins. They are routinely left out of reference books on Chinese charms: they were placed in the mouths of the dead, and collectors find them unlucky and disturbing.

In the Sahara the metal marks a grade. The Agadez cross has two forms, and the cross-shaped tanaghilt is made of stone or copper, while talhakim are cut from platelets of copper, aluminium or other metal; the blacksmiths who make the silver ones cast by lost wax and never hammer the metal. Han China graded the same way in wire: an emperor's jade burial suit is sewn with gold, princes and marquises with silver, their children with copper, and lesser aristocrats with silk thread.

Mesopotamia used bronze where the stakes were highest. Pazuzu heads are chiefly terracotta but also bronze, and ritual texts from Uruk state that a woman could be given a bronze necklace or amulet of Pazuzu to protect her from miscarrying through the interference of Lamashtu. In Anatolia copper is named as an ingredient rather than a shape: the account of the eye bead gives it as "a mixture of molten glass, iron, copper, water, and salt, ingredients that are thought to shield people from evil".

Europe's luckiest object started in bronze and only later turned to iron. In 1897 four bronze horseshoes with what are apparently nail holes were found in an Etruscan tomb dated around 400 BCE, and around 1000 CE cast bronze horseshoes with nail holes became common in Europe, with a scalloped outer rim and six nail holes; the widespread manufacture of iron horseshoes belongs to the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Copper's colour shows up in Egypt too, where copper pigments give faience its blue-green lustre, and copper, like lead, was imported in prosperous periods when local ores ran short.

Inference — copper's real advantage is archaeological rather than protective. Iron was valuable and worn-out items were generally reforged and reused, which makes early iron charms hard to find; bronze was cast, buried and left. What looks like a preference for bronze in the record may be a preference for bronze in the ground.

How it is made and carried

Cast in a mould, or beaten into a thin sheet — a lamella — and inscribed. Roy Kotansky's corpus of inscribed protective sheets is titled Greek Magical Amulets: the inscribed gold, silver, copper and bronze lamellae, which is the shortest statement of what this metal was used for in the Greco-Roman world.

Now

Copper-alloy charms are still cast and still collected, and the collecting is where the arguments are. The Chinese burial charm is the clearest case in this atlas of an object excluded from the published record for reasons that have nothing to do with the object: it is complete, dated, measured, inscribed, and left out of the reference books because of where it sat in a corpse's mouth.

materialsBone and ivory

Two related things a charm gets cut from: bone, from any animal including a human one, and ivory, meaning tusk — from African and Asian elephants above all, and also from hippopotamus, walrus, narwhal and mammoth. Both carve finely, take a polish, and yellow with handling. Both come out of a body, and every tradition that uses them says something about whose body it was.

The story

Bone is on the first list of Egyptian amulet materials: faience above all, then stone, metal, bone, wood and gold. It is still on the list four thousand years later and half a world away. Thai amulets are made from plaster, metal, wood or bone, sometimes with ash, monk's hair or temple relics worked into them. The palad khik, the Thai phallic charm, is made from wood, metal, bone, horn or ivory by monks who specialise in them, and engraving its inscriptions can take days.

In the Pacific the material is a tooth as much as a tusk. The lei niho palaoa, worn at the neck by Hawaiian ali'i of both sexes, is in its nineteenth-century form a whale tooth carved into a hook and hung on plaited human hair; precontact examples were under two inches long and were also made from shell, bone, wood, stone and coral. In Greenland a tupilaq was made by an angakkuq from animal parts — bone, skin, hair, sinew — and, in the accounts that survive, from parts taken from the corpses of children.

Inference — bone and ivory sit on the opposite side of the survival line from cloth and herb. They last in the ground, so they are heavily represented in museum trays, and they last in a collection, so they are heavily represented in the ethics literature too. Those are the same fact seen twice.

Now

Ivory is the material in this atlas with the hardest live constraint on it. Asian elephant ivory was barred from international trade by a CITES Appendix One listing in 1975; the African elephant was moved to Appendix One in October 1989, enacted in January 1990. Through the 1980s about 75,000 African elephants a year were killed for the trade, and the African elephant population fell from roughly 1.3 million to 600,000 between 1979 and 1989. China ended its domestic ivory trade and processing, with commercial activity halting on 31 March 2017. The UK Ivory Act 2018 received Royal Assent on 20 December 2018. A museum drawer of ivory charms is therefore a drawer of objects that in most cases cannot lawfully be traded now, whatever they were when they were carved.

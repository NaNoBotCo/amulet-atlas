traditionsMesopotamian household protection

said the apotropaic craft of Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia and Assyria

also: Mesopotamian apotropaic magic · Babylonian white magic · Assyrian household magic

traditionMesopotamiaThe LevantIranIraqSyriaIranTurkeyconfidence mediumneeds checking

A craft with four moving parts: a demon with a name, a counter-power with a name, an incantation that addresses the demon directly, and a physical object — a plaque, a cast head, a figurine — placed where the danger comes in. Apotropaic ritual of this kind was practised throughout the ancient Near East, in houses rather than in state temples.

The story

The method is verbal before it is material. The incantations open by naming the demon and then work through her epithets — 'Dimme, daughter of Anu', 'Who was named by the gods' — because the names are the hold. The object carries the words. An amulet with Lamashtu's image on it is not a portrait; it is her, held and instructed.

The counter-power does not have to be good. Pazuzu is a wind that brings plague and a text addresses him as 'Agony of Mankind', and his head hangs over the bed anyway, because he is the one thing that sends Lamashtu back underground. That is the tradition's most distinctive move: it hires demons.

Placement is documented rather than guessed. Amulets bearing Lamashtu's image could be worn, attached to the body or to clothing, or set near beds, doorways and other vulnerable spaces. A large Pazuzu wall amulet was found on the floor of the main reception room of a house at Dur-Katlimmu, where it had hung facing the entryway. Ritual texts from Uruk prescribe a bronze Pazuzu necklace for a woman at risk of miscarriage.

The objects were made at scale. The uniformity of the Pazuzu heads and their number across sites is read as mass production, which makes this the earliest industrialised amulet trade in the atlas.

Inference — the tradition does not end where the language does. Six hundred years after the last Pazuzu heads, the same ground is producing incantation bowls: a named demon, an incantation addressed to it directly, an object buried at the threshold. The scripts are Aramaic and Mandaic and the demons have new names, and the method is the one described above.

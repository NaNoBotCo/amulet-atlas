amuletJapanJapan

A flat talisman issued by a shrine or a temple: a sheet of paper, a thin rectangular plaque in a paper sleeve, or a wooden tablet, carrying the name of the shrine or of the deity enshrined there and stamped with the shrine's seal. Paper ones are kamifuda, wooden ones kifuda. It is not carried on a person. It stands on a household altar, or goes on a wall above eye level, or is fixed at a gate, a doorway, a kitchen or a ceiling.

The story

Two lines run into the ofuda. One is Daoist: the lingfu, the talismanic script of China, which reached Japan through Onmyōdō. The other is print — the woodblock sheets of Buddhist text and image that temples were turning out from the Nara and Heian periods on. In the medieval period the three Kumano shrines in Wakayama stamped their paper talismans with stylised crows and called them Kumano Goōfu, Ox King Talismans; oaths and contracts were written on the blank side, so the sheet held the agreement and the sanction at once.

The standard modern form comes from Ise. Wandering preachers attached to the Ise shrines, the oshi, handed out purification wands called Oharai Taima as a sign that prayers had been said on a devotee's behalf. The wand came folded into paper shaped like a sword blade — kenharai — or boxed. Distribution started in the Muromachi period and peaked in the Edo: a document of 1777 records that eighty-nine to ninety per cent of households in the country held an Ise talisman. That is close to universal coverage, three centuries before mass media.

Then the state took it over. An imperial decree of 1871 abolished the oshi and moved production of the talisman, renamed Jingū Taima, to the shrine's own offices. In 1900 a new Department of Priests, the Kanbesho, took over; in 1927 the National Association of Shinto Priests; after the Second World War, the Association of Shinto Shrines, which still distributes Jingū Taima to affiliated shrines across Japan. Inference — an object whose issuing body is renamed four times in seventy-five years is an object the state has an opinion about.

How it is made and carried

Received at a shrine or temple counter, in exchange for hatsuhoryō, a first-fruit offering rather than a price. Shinsatsu are set on a kamidana, a household Shinto altar, or on a stand; failing that, upright in a clean space above eye level, facing east, south or south-east. The Association of Shinto Shrines recommends three per household: the Jingū Taima from Ise, the ofuda of the tutelary kami of where you live, and the ofuda of a shrine you are personally attached to. In a three-door altar the Ise one goes in the middle. Other ofuda go elsewhere in the house — hearth deities in the kitchen, a talisman of the Buddhist figure Ucchuṣma in the toilet, the horned Tsuno Daishi, the Tendai monk Ryōgen drawn as a yaksha, on a doorway. They are replaced annually, ideally before the year turns, and the old ones are burnt at the Sagichō or Dondoyaki fire in mid-January. Most shrines will take back another shrine's ofuda; many will not take a Buddhist one.

cited

Now

Issued year-round by shrines and temples with permanent staff, and distributed nationally through the Association of Shinto Shrines as of 2026. Japan has around 100,000 public shrines, about 80,000 of them affiliated with the Association and roughly 20,000 unaffiliated, which is the network the Ise talisman travels down.

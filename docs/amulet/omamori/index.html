amuletsOmamori御守

said o-MA-mo-ri

also: o-mamori · お守り · Japanese shrine charm

amuletJapanJapan

A small brocade pouch, a few centimetres tall, drawn shut at the top with a cord and printed with the name of the shrine or temple that issued it. Inside is a slip of paper or a chip of wood carrying a prayer or an invocation. The pouch stays shut: nobody who carries one opens it. Most hang from a bag, a school satchel, a phone strap or a car mirror rather than round the neck.

The story

Omamori are issued by Shinto shrines and by Buddhist temples alike, dedicated to particular kami or to Buddhist figures, and sold to anyone regardless of what they believe. The shrine consecrates the contents by rite; a Shinto one is described as holding busshin, spiritual offshoots of the deity, a Buddhist one kesshin, manifestations.

The folklorist Yanagita Kunio dated the thing a reader now recognises to the Tokugawa period at the earliest. Writing in 1969 he put it this way: "Japanese have probably always believed in amulets of one kind or another, but the modern printed charms now given out by shrines and temples first became popular in the Tokugawa period or later, and the practice of a person wearing miniature charms is also new. The latter custom is particularly common in cities." Two claims sit in that sentence — the printed charm is early modern, the pocket-sized charm is later still, and urban.

The demand outgrew the shrines. Where a popular shrine or temple cannot keep up, it orders omamori from a factory, and priests have objected to the quality and to what they call the "authenticity" of the factory product. Both halves of that get reported here: the factory pouch and the hand-finished one come off the same counter, and the argument over whether they are the same object is an argument inside the tradition.

Inference — a charm designed to be replaced every twelve months is a charm with a business model, and the New Year crowds are the year's trade. Fushimi Inari-taisha alone drew 2.69 million worshippers over three days at the New Year in 2006, a police figure and the largest in western Japan. No source this project has read publishes what any shrine takes in omamori money, so no figure is given here.

How it is made and carried

Received, not bought — the verb the tradition uses is sazukaru, to be granted, and the money handed over is hatsuhoryō, a first-fruit offering. If a visitor cannot find one for their need, they may ask a priest to have one made; if enough people ask for the same one, the shrine starts stocking it. The pouch is then carried, tied to a bag or a strap, and not opened; opening it loses what is in it. It is customarily swapped for a new one once a year. The old one goes back to the shrine or temple it came from, usually on or just after New Year, and is burnt rather than thrown out, at the Sagichō or Dondoyaki fire held at the Little New Year on 14 or 15 January. Most Shinto shrines will accept another shrine's omamori; many will not accept a Buddhist one, and the reverse holds too.

cited

Now

Still issued by nearly every shrine and temple in Japan as of 2026, in a spread of categories a buyer picks from: kōtsū-anzen for the road, gakugyō-jōju for exams, shōbai-hanjō for trade, en-musubi for finding a partner, anzan for a safe delivery, kanai-anzen for the household, yaku-yoke against a bad year. Alongside them, shops sell omamori-shaped goods with Mickey Mouse, Hello Kitty, Snoopy or Kewpie on them, issued by no shrine and consecrated by nobody — a separate product that borrows the shape.

cited

Not to be confused with

  • Ofuda — an ofuda is a flat tablet or sheet that stands on a household altar or goes on a wall; an omamori is a closed pouch that travels with a person
  • Omikuji — an omikuji is a loose paper strip with a graded fortune printed on it, drawn at random; an omamori is sewn shut and chosen by category

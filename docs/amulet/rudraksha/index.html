amuletsRudrakshaरुद्राक्ष

said rud-RAAK-sha

also: rudraksha · rudrākṣa · blueberry bead

amuletSouth AsiaThe Himalaya and TibetIsland Southeast AsiaIndiaNepalIndonesianeeds checking

A hard brown seed-stone, ridged from top to bottom, drilled and strung at the throat. The stone is the pit of the fruit of Elaeocarpus ganitrus, a fast-growing evergreen of the Himalayan foothills; ripe, the fruit wears a blue husk, which is why sellers call the bead a blueberry bead. The ridges are read as faces — mukha, Sanskrit for face — and the count of faces on one bead is the first thing a buyer looks for. Nepalese beads run 20 to 35 mm, Indonesian ones 5 to 25 mm.

The story

The bead belongs to Shiva, and the texts say why. Several late-medieval Upanishads give a scene: the sage Bhusunda asks Kalagni Rudra where rudraksha begin, and the god answers that he closed his eyes to destroy the Tripurasura, drops fell from the closed eyes to the earth, and those tears became the stones. In the same passage the promise attached to the bead is not protection but merit — the god says that uttering the name alone earns the benefit of giving ten cows in charity, and seeing and touching it doubles that.

The Tamil Tirumurai reads the bead as a mark you can see across a street: those who walk the paths of charya and kriya wear twin rings in the earlobes and a twin rudraksha garland at the neck, and the text calls that a sign of piety.

The counts are specific and published. A bead may carry between one and twenty-one faces; instances of twenty-six or twenty-seven have been reported and are rare. One face, ekamukha, is the rarest of all. A rudraksha with eleven faces is worn by renunciants, a two-faced bead by the married, and a five-faced bead stands for Hanuman. Naturally joined beads have their own names — savar for a conjoined pair, Gauri Shankar where the two are of a size, Garbha Gauri where one is smaller, Ganesha where a bead carries a trunk-like protrusion, Trijuti for three grown together.

Inference — every one of those distinctions is a price. A tradition that sorts a seed into twenty-one grades, then again by how many grew together, has built the apparatus a market needs, and the rarest grades are the ones a buyer cannot check by eye.

How it is made and carried

Strung as a mala — a garland — usually of 108 beads plus one, which the wearers call the meru, bindu or guru bead and use to mark where a cycle of recitation begins and ends. Shorter strings run 27+1 and 54+1. Silk or black or red cotton thread is the common stringing; jewellers less often use copper, silver or gold wire, and a bead strung too tightly can split. The Devi Bhagavata Purana describes how a garland is prepared. The recitation the beads count is most often Om Namah Shivaya.

cited

Now

Grown and sold as a crop. Cultivation centres on the Himalayan foothills in Nepal and India — Kathmandu and the Arun Valley in Nepal, Kulu and Rameshwaram in India — with groves in Uttarakhand, and a 184-acre grove, the Ch. Devi Lal Rudraksha Vatika in Yamunanagar district, Haryana, is planted to the tree along with some 400 endangered ayurvedic herbs. Nepal, India and Indonesia are the three sources named for the jewellery trade as of 2026, and the bead is priced alongside semi-precious stones.

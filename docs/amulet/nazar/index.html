A handmade glass bead: concentric circles, or a teardrop, in dark blue, white, light blue and black, sometimes rimmed in yellow or gold. The Turkish boncuk is blue glass with a blue, white and black dot set on a white or yellow centre. It hangs at a neck, on a wrist, over a door, from a mirror, above a till, on a cradle.

Arabic naẓar, 'sight' or 'looking' — the same word for the look that does the harm and for the bead that answers it; Turkish nazar boncuğu adds boncuk, Turkic for 'bead'
The word travels: Albanian, Azerbaijani, Bengali, Hebrew, Hindi–Urdu, Kurdish, Pashto, Persian, Punjabi, Sindhi and Turkish all use it. Greek calls the object máti, 'eye'. In India and Pakistan the phrase nazar lag gai says somebody has been hit by it. cited

The story

The bead is older than any of the countries that sell it. Glass eye beads are documented and extant from as early as the 16th century BCE, and the design spread with glassmaking itself — Mesopotamia to Egypt, Carthage to Greece, Phoenicia to Persia, and right through the Roman imperial period.

The explanations people give for the colour are specific. "According to Turkish belief, blue acts as a shield against evil and even absorbs negativity." And the materials are part of the claim, not just the manufacture: "The bead is made of a mixture of molten glass, iron, copper, water, and salt, ingredients that are thought to shield people from evil." A second explanation in the cited account runs the other way round — in the Middle East and the Mediterranean, blue eyes are relatively rare, so people with light eyes were held able to curse with a look, and the Assyrians already had turquoise and blue-eye amulets.

The bead also has a rule for its own death. If one breaks, a very strong nazar has hit the wearer, and the bead stored it all up and broke in order to protect them. That is the only retirement instruction in this batch that the object gives itself.

cited

Now

Mass-produced and sold everywhere: the Grand Bazaar in Istanbul, Monastiraki in Athens, airport shops, phone cases. A Turkish budget airline, Fly Air, carried the nazar boncuğu on the tail fins of its aircraft. The bead was added to Unicode as U+1F9FF 🧿 NAZAR AMULET in 2018, which makes it one of the very few amulets in this atlas that a reader can send in a message. Whether a factory bead is the same object as a lampworked one is argued by sellers and buyers alike; this atlas reports the argument and describes both the same way.

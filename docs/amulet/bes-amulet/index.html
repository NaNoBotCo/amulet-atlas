A squat bearded figure with a lion's face, a feathered crown, a protruding tongue and bandy legs, shown full-face when every other Egyptian god is shown in profile. He is ithyphallic, sometimes in a soldier's tunic, sometimes brandishing a knife. He comes as an amulet, as a mask, moulded onto faience baby bottles, cut into the legs of furniture and headrests, painted on storage jars, and inked into skin.

The story

Bes faces you. That is the whole design argument: Egyptian gods are drawn in profile, and Bes is drawn head-on, dressed for a fight, so that anything approaching the house meets him looking straight at it.

He is a household god, not a temple one. What he is for is specific and domestic — killing snakes, driving off evil spirits, watching children, and standing with Taweret at a birth to fight off what comes for women in labour. Because he drove off harm he also came to stand for the good things: music, dance, sexual pleasure.

His origin is southern. People in Upper Egypt venerated him long before Lower Egypt did, his sub-Saharan features and his feathered crown point at Kush or the C-Group, and Donald Mackenzie in 1907 called him a Middle Kingdom import from Nubia whose cult only spread from the start of the New Kingdom. That story has since been complicated: thirteen Bes-like figurines were found at Tell el-Farkha in deposits from the predynastic Naqada period.

He travelled further than most. His image reaches Syria in the north and the Balearic Islands in the west, then the Roman and Achaemenid empires. From the end of the sixth century BCE Bes spreads across the Achaemenid empire while Egypt is part of it; he turns up at Susa and as far as central Asia, and over time gets redrawn in Persian clothes and a Persian headdress. Beset, the female form, was more popular on Minoan Crete; the male form took with the Phoenicians and the Cypriots.

He was also worn under the skin. A wall painting from the workmen's village at Deir el-Medina, Nineteenth Dynasty, shows a dancing Bes tattooed on the thigh of a female musician playing her instrument; New Kingdom tattoos of Bes are recorded on the thighs of dancers, musicians and servant girls. A female mummy from Aksha in Nubia, fourth century BCE, carries a Bes on her thigh.

He lasted. In the Ptolemaic period chambers were built at Saqqara painted with Bes and Beset, which Egyptologists take to have been for curing fertility problems or for general healing. A Bes-vase of the second century BCE has been found to carry traces of Peganum harmala and of the blue water lily, Nymphaea nouchali var. caerulea. The latest evidence the source names is a jug showing Bes from Tell Edfu, dated to the tenth century CE — Coptic Egypt, long after the temples closed.

How it is made and carried

Moulded in faience, carved, or painted on. Kept in the house rather than worn only on the body: a statue set up as a doorkeeper, a face on the jar, a face on the bed, a face on the baby's bottle. Tattooed on the thigh in the New Kingdom. Nothing in the sources read here restricts who may own one.

cited

Now

Not made as a household god. Bes turns up now as a museum object, as a character in fiction and games, and as a tattoo design. As of 2026 this project has read no account of anyone standing a Bes at a door for what the Egyptians stood him there for.

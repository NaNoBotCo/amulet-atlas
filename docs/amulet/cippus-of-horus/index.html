said SIP-us, plural cippi, from the Latin for a small pillar or marker

also: magical stela · healing stela · Horus on the Crocodiles · Horus stela · Horus cippus

amuletAncient EgyptEgyptconfidence mediumneeds checking

A slab of stone carved with the child Horus standing on two crocodiles, gripping snakes and scorpions in his fists, and often an antelope by the horns and a lion by the tail. Rows of hieroglyphs cover the rest of it. Sizes run from house-sized stelae down to amulets small enough to carry: the Metternich Stela is one block of greywacke about 35.8 inches tall and 13.25 wide, and the Metropolitan Museum also holds a miniature.

The story

This is an amulet you drink. The instruction is on the object: water is poured over the carved spells and the carved figures, collected at the base, and given to the person who has been bitten or stung, to swallow or to wash the wound with. The words come off the stone into the water. Priests recited alongside.

The Metternich Stela is the fullest of them. It dates to about 380–342 BCE, the Thirtieth Dynasty, under Nectanebo II, and it carries thirteen spells against venomous bites and wounds and against the illnesses they cause — including the story of Isis and the Seven Scorpions, which is among the most complete versions found on any monument. The logic is precedent: Horus as a child was stung in the marshes at Khemmis and was cured, and the spell puts the patient in the child's place.

It disappeared for two thousand years and turned up in a wall excavated in a Franciscan monastery. Muhammad Ali Pasha gave it to Prince Metternich in 1828; it sat at Kynžvart Castle in Bohemia until 1950, when the Metropolitan Museum of Art acquired it.

The class runs from the Third Intermediate Period to the end of Ptolemaic rule in Egypt. Not all of them are museum monuments — some are amulet-sized, and the same image of Horus on the crocodiles is carried. The Egyptian Museum in Cairo holds the healing statue of Djedhor, the Louvre a Priest of Bastet statue, and the Brooklyn Museum another cippus; Keith Seele, Robert Ritner and Jane Draycott have written on them.

Inference — an amulet that is used up by being washed is a different kind of object from one that is worn, and it puts this stone closer to an Ethiopian scroll read aloud, or to a Qur'anic bowl rinsed and drunk, than to the faience beetle in the next tray.

How it is made and carried

Carved in stone with the Horus figure on the front and the spells around it. Water is poured over it, caught, and drunk or applied to the body by the person suffering the poisoning, with rites recited by priests. The large ones stay where they stand; the small ones are carried.

cited

Now

Not in use. Cippi are museum objects — the Metropolitan Museum shows them at The Met Fifth Avenue, and the Brooklyn Museum, the Louvre and the Egyptian Museum in Cairo hold others. As of 2026 this project has read no account of water being poured over one for a patient.

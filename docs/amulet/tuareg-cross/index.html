amuletWest AfricaThe MaghrebNigerconfidence mediumneeds checking

A cast pendant, usually silver, with a ring or loop at the top and a flared, spreading body below — sometimes a cross, more often a shape between a cross and a shield. Blacksmiths cast it by the lost-wax process and never hammer the metal. The plate-shaped kind, talhakim, is cut from soft stone or from platelets of copper, aluminium or another metal; the tanaghilt is made in stone or copper.

cited

Tamashek tanaghilt / tasagalt, 'cast in a mould' — the name is a description of the method, not of a shape. Talhakim covers jewellery resembling a plate or a plate shield. Zakkat is the general Tamashek word for pendants; kaulé is widespread across the Sahel.
The European name calls it a cross after the town of Agadez. Only a few of the pieces actually resemble one. cited

The story

This is the best-known category of Saharan Berber jewellery, made especially by the Tuareg of Niger — and its use is narrower than its fame. Current use of tanaghilt and talhakim is restricted to a few Tuareg groups, the Kel Aïr and the Kel Geress, and to non-Berber peoples across Niger who have taken it up: Songhai, Zarma, Fulani, Hausa. Other Tuareg barely use them. Worn as ornaments, the pieces hang at the neck; a tanaghilt is also fixed to a woman's veil or to her forehead, and fixed upside down.

Ask the people wearing them what the shape means and the answers go vague. The source says so flatly: any question about their symbolism put to the users generally elicits only vague answers. Some name it a grigri; some call it a reserve of wealth.

The ethnologists Germaine Dieterlen and Ziedonis Ligers recorded one occasion that does give the object a sentence of its own. A father handed the jewel to his son at the age of virility, marriage and nomadism, and said: "My son, I give you the four directions of the world, because we do not know where you will go to die."

A Tuareg elder's narration gives another origin. A young nomad wanted to declare his love to a girl shut up at home and out of reach. The village blacksmith had an important place in Tuareg society and the right to enter the houses of all the families he traded with, so the young man had a jewel forged that combined the two syllables of the Tamashek word tara, love, written ⵜⵔ in Tifinagh, and sent the blacksmith in with it.

Tradition holds — the image may go back to ancient stylised images of the Carthaginian goddess Tanit. The source offers that as a possibility and this atlas leaves it there.

How it is made and carried

Cast by a blacksmith in silver by the lost-wax process — a wax model, a mould, the wax melted out, the metal poured in — and never hammered. The stone and platelet kinds are cut. Worn at the neck, or fixed to a woman's veil or forehead, upside down.

cited

Now

Made and worn, and also the standard thing a visitor to Niger brings home. As of 2026 this project has read no figure for the split between the two, and no source that says Tuareg smiths object to either.

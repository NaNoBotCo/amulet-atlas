amuletsTefillinתְּפִלִּין

said tfi-LIN; singular tefillah

also: phylacteries · shel yad · shel rosh

amuletThe LevantDiasporaNorth AmericaIsraelUnited States of America

Two small black leather boxes with long black straps. The arm box goes on the upper non-dominant arm and its strap winds down the forearm, round the hand and round the middle finger; the head box sits between the eyes at the hairline. Each box is made from a single piece of hide, cubical, on a wider base, with a passage at the back for the strap. On both sides of the head box the letter shin is moulded — with four branches on the wearer's left instead of three. The head strap's knot forms a dalet, the arm strap's a yud; with the shin those three letters spell Shaddai. Inside are parchment scrolls with four Torah passages: Exodus 13:1–10, Exodus 13:11–16, Deuteronomy 6:4–9 and Deuteronomy 11:13–21. Three thousand one hundred and eighty-eight letters, and a scribe can take as long as fifteen hours to write a set.

Mishnaic Hebrew plural of tefillah; Jastrow connects it to Syriac tefiltha, 'attachments' or 'hangings'. In the Hebrew Bible the objects are totafot, 'headband' or 'frontlet', from a reduplicated root ṭ-p-ṭ-p meaning 'to encircle'
The resemblance to Hebrew tefillah, 'prayer', is coincidental. The English word phylactery is from Greek phylaktērion, 'guarded post, safeguard, security', and in later Greek 'amulet' or 'charm' — it enters European languages from its single appearance in Matthew 23, not from anything Jewish usage calls these.
Folk etymology, labelled as one: Rabbi Akiva, in Sanhedrin 4b, read totafot as two foreign words for 'two' — tot in Coptic and fot in 'Afriki' — making 'two and two', for the four compartments of the head tefillah. The cited article calls this an early attempt at etymology. cited

The story

Like the mezuzah, tefillin are a commandment and not an amulet, and the tradition guards that distinction itself. The cited account puts it exactly: tefillin are sometimes mentioned together with amulets in the Talmud because of certain similarities of form, but they are never identified as such — they are specifically differentiated from them. Maimonides counts the arm box and the head box as two separate positive commandments. His account of what they do is about the wearer's mind: as long as they are on the head and the arm of a man, "he is modest and God-fearing and will not be attracted by hilarity or idle talk". The Sefer ha-Chinuch, in the 14th century, adds that they help subjugate worldly desire. Joseph Caro, in the 16th, explains the positions: next to the heart and above the brain, to show both organs willing to serve.

The argument that they are also apotropaic is made, and made by named people. Yehudah B. Cohn argues that tefillin should be read as an invented tradition, aimed at countering the popularity of Greek amulets with a Jewish one. Joshua Trachtenberg took every ornament worn on the body, whatever its declared function, to have begun as an amulet. And the early rabbinic sources are explicit enough on their own: Numbers Rabbah 12:3 has tefillin defeating "a thousand demons" coming up on the left side; rabbis Yohanan and Nahman use their sets to repel the demons of privies; Elisha the Winged, scrupulous in this mitzvah, is saved from Roman persecution. Tefillin are also credited with lengthening life, and are listed in one breath with objects that are amuletic in nature.

So the atlas includes them and states the objection rather than dissolving it. The object is dated: Jews were wearing physical tefillin by at least the first century CE, which is shown by the finds at Qumran and by the reference in Matthew 23. Karaite Judaism reads the commandment as metaphor and does not.

Who wears them is governed. The obligation rests on Jewish males from thirteen; women are exempt, as they are from most time-bound positive commandments, and since Rema in the 16th century the prevalent practice among religious Jews has strongly discouraged women from wearing them. Claims that Rashi's daughters and the wife of Chaim ibn Attar wore tefillin have no historical evidence behind them. In Orthodox practice it remains a male obligation; Conservative women often wrap; SAR High School in Riverdale allowed girls to wrap at morning prayer from 2013, probably the first Modern Orthodox high school in the United States to do so; a Jerusalem District Court judge ruled in 2013 that Women of the Wall wearing tefillin at the Western Wall did not violate 'local custom'; in 2018 students at Hebrew College in Boston made a series of videos teaching female and transgender Jews to wrap.

How it is made and carried

Cubical boxes from a single piece of hide, in three grades — peshutim, made with several pieces of parchment forming the inner walls of the head box; dakkot, stretched from one thin piece; and the more durable gassot. Painted black by custom now, though the archaeological finds do not show that this was always so. Straps must be black on the outside and any colour but red inside. The four passages go on one strip in the arm box, which has a single compartment, and on four separate scrolls in the head box, which has four — because the verses name the hand device in the singular and the head device in the plural. Written by a scribe in Ashuri script in one of three lettering styles: Beis Yosef for Ashkenazim, Arizal for Hasidim, Velish for Sefardim. The letters must be written in order; a mistake found later cannot be corrected, because the replacement would be out of sequence, and the set is invalid. Talmudic commentators disagreed on the order of the scrolls: Rashi held to the order the passages appear in the Torah, Rabbeinu Tam switched the last two. Most follow Rashi; some also lay Rabbeinu Tam briefly, a custom of the Ari taken up by Hasidim, many Sephardic communities and some Ashkenazim. The Vilna Gaon, who wore Rashi's, rejected the stringency, pointing out that there are 64 possible arrangements and one cannot put on 64 sets. Worn during Shacharit on weekdays and removed after morning services; Jewish men once wore them all day.

Now

Worn daily wherever weekday morning prayer is kept, as of 2026. The live question inside the tradition is gender, and it is being answered differently by different communities — see the story above, which names the rulings and the dates.

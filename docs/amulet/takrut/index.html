This atlas prints what the sources publish about the object and does not print a consecration formula. — Thai amulet practice as described in the sources: the incantations depend on the creator's lineage in each school

The atlas prints the restriction and stops. Why.

A tube. A thin sheet of metal or a strip of palm leaf is inscribed with yant — sacred geometry and Pali phrases — then rolled tight into an elongated scroll and bound with cord, or sealed in a case. Nothing on the outside shows what is inside. It is tied to the body on a cord at the waist, hung at the chest on a chain, or worn like any other piece of jewellery. Some are small enough to hold between the teeth.

The story

The takrut is the amulet Thai literature reaches for first. It is the form mentioned more often than any other in Khun Chang Khun Phaen, the Thai epic, and the source this record follows puts it in Thai culture before Buddhism arrived, with the monk-made takrut becoming the earliest form of Buddhist amulet around 1200 to 1300 CE. That last claim carries no citation in the source, so it is dated here with a flag rather than a full stop.

What goes on the sheet is yant — yantra — incantations and geometric designs carrying Pali gatha and Buddhist invocations. The script is usually Khom Pali, the variant of Khmer script used in Thailand; in the north, Lan Na Tai Tham. The same writing appears on cloth, on metal plates and in skin, so a takrut is one surface among several for a single body of design.

The demand was practical. In older Thailand, with constant warfare and poor security, takrut were blessed mainly for safe keeping — invulnerability — and issued to people going to work, to travel on business, or to war.

The positions carry meanings. A takrut is worn about a part of the body to protect that part, or to grant it power. The one held between the teeth is for speech: the power is called sariga, after the golden-tongued celestial magpie of Vedic and Thai Buddhist legend, and it makes the carrier a stronger speaker.

How it is made and carried

Inscribe the yant on metal foil — or on palm leaf — roll it into a tube, bind it with cord, case it. Which yant, and what is said over it, belongs to the maker's lineage. Thai amulet rules place the takrut outside the Buddha-image restrictions: because it carries no monk and no Buddha image, it may be put in a trouser pocket, where an amulet bearing the Buddha may not go below the waist at all. Worn at the waist or at the chest, the purpose is the same either way — protection of the person carrying it.

cited

Now

Still made, still sold, still worn, and sold from the same trays as the pressed Buddha tablets at Tha Phrachan in Bangkok, as of the accounts this project has read. Online dealers list them by monk, by temple and by year in the same way collectors grade a phra khrueang.

inference

Not to be confused with

  • Phra khrueang — a takrut is a plain rolled tube with nothing to look at; a phra khrueang carries an image in relief
  • Ta'wiz — both are rolled written charms in a case — a takrut carries yant in Khom Pali, a taweez carries Arabic

amuletAotearoa New ZealandThe PacificNew Zealand

A pendant of a human figure, head tilted, hands on thigh and chest or both on the thighs, carved from pounamu and suspended on a plaited cord fastened with a loop and toggle. Pounamu is the Māori term for several hard, tough stones found only in the South Island — nephrite jade in the varieties Māori classify by colour as kahurangi, īnanga and kawakawa, and the translucent bowenite called tangiwai. A hei-tiki is a taonga: a treasure with whakapapa, an ancestral line of its own.

The story

Hei-tiki are, in Te Papa's summary of Māori adornment, the most famous and most highly prized of Māori personal ornaments. Two readings of the figure are given by Horatio Gordon Robley in A History of the Maori Tiki: that they are memorials to ancestors, or that they represent Hineteiwaiwa, the goddess of childbirth. Both have practices attached. A hei-tiki was often buried when its kaitiaki, its guardian, died, and retrieved later to be brought out at tangihanga, the mourning. And because of the connection with Hineteiwaiwa, hei-tiki were often given to a woman by her husband's family if she was having trouble conceiving.

The mana of one is not in the carving. Dougal Austin's 2014 thesis, supervised by Peter Adds and based on Te Papa's hei-tiki collection with early-contact pieces in overseas collections, finds that the mana of hei-tiki comes from the "agency of prolonged ancestral use", and that the form was "highly developed ... from the outset to conform to adze-shaped pieces of pounamu". Pounamu taonga gain mana as they pass between generations; the stone is held to take on the mana of past owners, and heirloom pieces are named after former owners. That is why the most prized taonga are the ones with known histories running back many generations, and why they were given to seal agreements.

The collections are large and their history is not neutral. Te Papa holds over 200 hei-tiki and the British Museum about 50; some were exchanged or gifted to European travellers and sailors at the earliest point of contact, and, in Wikipedia's words, "the majority were stolen or improperly obtained through colonial processes".

One story sits oddly beside the rest and is reported here as somebody else's reading, not this atlas's. The captain of HMS New Zealand, a battlecruiser funded by the New Zealand government in 1911, wore a hei-tiki and a piupiu into three First World War battles; the crew attributed the ship's lack of casualties to it and called her a lucky ship. That is a Pākehā crew's account of a taonga, and it is what a taonga becomes when it is read by people outside the whakapapa it belongs to.

In 1997 the Crown returned ownership of all naturally occurring pounamu to Ngāi Tahu, the South Island iwi, under the Ngāi Tahu Claims Settlement.

How it is made and carried

The stone was first cut roughly as a small adze — pounamu is hard and valuable enough that a carver removes as little as possible, which is why the head tilts. It is then smoothed by abrasive rubbing, shaped and bored with sticks and water, and polished over a long time. Finished, it is hung on a plaited cord with a loop and toggle. It is not retired; it is inherited, and its history is the point of it.

cited

Now

Carved by Māori artists, including collectives set up by iwi that export jewellery and carvings, and worn by Māori and by other New Zealanders as a mark of identity. Retailers also sell visitor versions in jade, other stone, plastic and other materials; the Wikipedia account of pounamu notes that much of the carved "greenstone" in souvenir shops is jade sourced overseas. As of 2026 the two trades run side by side, and this atlas reports the difference without ranking the buyers.

amuletRome and ItalyGreece and the AegeanItalyconfidence mediumneeds checking

A crescent moon on a chain, usually gold, worn by girls in ancient Rome. Isidore of Seville defines it exactly: "Lunulae are female ornaments in the likeness of the moon, little hanging gold bullae." It came off on the eve of her marriage, with her childhood toys and the rest of her child's clothes, and she put on women's dress.

The story

Two lines of Plautus are most of what a reader can hold onto. In Epidicus, the speaker asks the girl Telestis: "Don't you remember my bringing you a gold lunula on your birthday, and a little gold ring for your finger?" In Rudens, Palaestra says her father gave her a golden bulla on the day she was born. Girls got a crescent, boys got a locket, and the plays treat both as the ordinary furniture of a childhood.

The cited account is direct about what Romans wore amulets for: against evil forces, demons and sorcery, and especially against the evil eye. It also draws a map line worth keeping. Lunulae were common right across the Mediterranean; the bulla, the boys' version, was mostly an Italian object. This record lists Italy alone in its countries because Italy is where the source names the practice; the wider Mediterranean spread is stated in the source without countries attached to it.

Inference — an amulet with a removal ceremony is doing two jobs. It guards a child, and it counts. Take it off and something has ended — a wedding in this case, a toga in the boys'. The atlas has a small class of these, and the Bulgarian martenitsa, which comes off when the wearer sees a stork, belongs to it.

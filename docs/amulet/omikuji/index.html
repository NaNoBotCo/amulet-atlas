amuletsOmikuji御神籤

said o-mi-KU-ji

also: o-mikuji · おみくじ · sacred lot

amuletJapanJapan

A narrow strip of printed paper drawn at random from a box at a shrine or temple, for a small offering. It carries a graded verdict at the top — great blessing down to misfortune — then a run of short readings for particular departments of a life: auspicious directions, a wish, a person being waited for, a lost article, travel, business, study, market speculation, disputes, love, moving house, childbirth, illness, a marriage proposal. It is not a charm anybody wears. It is filed in this atlas because of what happens next: a bad slip is folded and tied to a pine branch or to a rack of wire in the grounds, and left behind. The disposal is the charm-act.

Japanese kuji 籤 'lot, drawn slip', with honorific mi- and o-; literally 'the sacred lot'
Kuji carries two senses at once: chance used to make a verdict impartial, and chance used to consult a deity's will. The same word covers drawing straws for an office and asking a kami. cited

The story

The dating here is unusually firm for a paper object. A wooden container of oracular lots dated 1409 survives at Tendai-ji in Iwate Prefecture, which puts the method in Japan before the Muromachi period. The system it belongs to was modelled on the Chinese kau chim — a tube of bamboo sticks read against a numbered sequence of printed oracles — and the hundred five-character quatrains used in Japanese Buddhist temples descend from a Southern Song set, the Tiānzhú língqiān.

Those hundred verses are attributed to Ryōgen (912–985), the Tendai monk known posthumously as Ganzan Daishi, and the sequence is called the Ganzan Daishi Hyakusen after him. Tradition holds — Avalokiteśvara revealed the verses to him. The attribution itself has a date and an author: Tenkai (1536–1643) is credited with attaching Ryōgen's name to the set, and a story from one of Tenkai's disciples has Ryōgen appear to Tenkai in a dream to reveal that the hundred quatrains, lost for centuries, still existed. Copies duly turned up at Togakushi Shrine in Shinano. The sequence spread through the Edo period into temples of every school, Tendai or not, and publishers put out books explaining what the oracles meant.

The pine is a pun. Matsu 松, pine, is a homophone of matsu 待つ, to wait: tie the bad fortune to the tree and the bad luck waits there instead of following you home. A good fortune can be tied up too, to make it work harder, or kept.

Omikuji still do public work beyond personal fortune. Provincial festivals use the same drawing to pick the tōnin, the person responsible for a coming year's religious duties: names go on slips, and the choice is settled by a ritual strike of a gohei wand.

How it is made and carried

Make a small offering, shake or draw from the box, take the slip you get. Read the grade first. The standard Ganzan Daishi sequence runs great blessing (dai-kichi), blessing (kichi), small blessing (shō-kichi), half-blessing (han-kichi), future blessing (sue-kichi), future small blessing (sue-shō-kichi), misfortune (kyō); other sequences add middle blessing, great misfortune, or a grade that says the outcome is still undetermined and depends on what you do. A bad slip is folded and knotted onto a pine or onto the wire racks set up for it. A good one is either tied up as well or pocketed.

cited

Now

Available at shrines and temples across Japan as of 2026; since 2024 vending machines dispense them in some places. The paper fortune in a Western fortune cookie may descend from this: the claim is made by Seiichi Kito of Fugetsu-Do, and rests on evidence that American fortune cookies derive from nineteenth-century Kyoto crackers called tsujiura senbei.
